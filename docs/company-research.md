How to read this document: every statement is labeled.

- **[Verified]** — stated on a public page (source cited). Short quotes are kept under 15 words.
- **[Observation]** — something I noticed by reviewing the pages (structure, presentation).
- **[Interpretation]** — my reading of what the facts mean for users and design.
- **[Assumption]** — something I believe but could not verify. Must be validated.

## 1. Company and positioning

- **[Verified]** Artemis presents itself as software for residential solar, roofing, batteries and home-improvement sales. Homepage headline: “Design, sell and execute at the speed of light.” (S1)
- **[Verified]** The site footer states that Artemis is a brand of MonaLee, Inc. (S1). The job posting is published under “Mona Lee Inc.” (S6)
- **[Verified]** The features page describes “a single platform built for speed, accuracy, and scale” that replaces “stitched-together tools.” (S2)
- **[Verified]** The job posting describes the company as founded in 2022, venture-backed, and using machine learning and AI. (S6)
- **[Verified]** The about page says the founders were frustrated by “slow quoting and endless manual tasks.” (S4)
- **[Observation]** Positioning emphasizes speed (“seconds”, “speed of light”) and consolidation (one platform instead of many tools).

## 2. Audiences named

- **[Verified]** Four role segments appear on the homepage and solutions page: **Designers, Sales, Operations, Owners.** (S1, S3)
- **[Verified]** Operations is described as tracking every project “from sale to PTO.” (S3)
- **[Verified]** The pricing page heading addresses “solar and roofing teams.” (S5)
- **[Verified]** The about page refers to “fast-growing local installers and national leaders.” (S4)

## 3. Product capabilities presented

- **[Verified]** Features listed: 3D AI designs, roofing measurements and reports, battery sizing, integrated financing, a “Sales Mode” for in-home or virtual selling, an API (including an iFrame option), storefronts, and home improvement. (S1, S2)
- **[Verified]** Named lender integrations include GoodLeap, Sunlight and others; CRM integrations include HubSpot, GoHighLevel and Salesforce; e-signature via DocuSign. (S2, FAQ on S1/S2)
- **[Verified]** The roof report is described in the FAQ as in development with early-access customers. (S2)
- **[Observation]** No numbered post-sale workflow is shown. The closest structure is a three-part framing: design faster, sell easier, operate better. (S1)

## 4. Customer problems as the company describes them

- **[Verified]** “clunky tools, long design turnaround times, and fragmented workflows.” (S4)
- **[Verified]** Operations framed as working “without manual chase-downs.” (S1)
- **[Interpretation]** The company’s own language points to coordination pain after the sale (chasing status across people and systems), even though most of the site’s detail is about the sales half.

## 5. Website structure and navigation

- **[Verified]** Top navigation: Features (dropdown), Solutions (dropdown), Blog, Pricing, About, Login, and a “Get a demo” call to action. (S1)
- **[Verified]** Homepage order (summarized): hero → rating badge → role section → customer logos → feature cards → three-step framing → roles → testimonials → stats → FAQ → final call to action → footer. (S1)
- **[Verified]** The product login is on a separate domain from the marketing site. (S1)
- **[Observation]** The primary call to action everywhere is a demo request; there is no self-serve trial. The FAQ says free trials are not offered “at this time.” (S1)

## 6. Pricing presentation

- **[Verified]** The pricing page is an interactive calculator by trade (Roofing, Solar, Solar + Roofing), monthly volume, term and payment method, with a per-design price. (S5)
- **[Verified]** It states $0 onboarding and unlimited users, and lists Pro, Grow and Enterprise plans in its FAQ. (S5)
- **[Observation]** Pricing is framed per design (unit economics for the installer) rather than per seat, which matches “unlimited users.”
- **[Interpretation]** Per-design pricing suggests the product is valued by throughput. More signed projects means more post-sale work for operations teams.

## 7. Visual identity and interaction

- **[Observation]** I reviewed page content in text form. I did **not** extract exact brand colors or font files, and this project does not reuse them. Groundwork’s visual language is original.
- **[Observation]** Feature sections use repeated card + “Get a demo” patterns. Statistics and third-party validation claims appear often.

## 8. Claims I did not use as facts

The site shows performance statistics, review scores and validation claims (for example design speed and accuracy figures). These are **company claims**. This project does not repeat them as facts and does not depend on them.

## 9. UX strengths and opportunities (my analysis, not company statements)

| | Note | Label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | Clear role-based segmentation helps visitors self-identify. | Observation |
| Strength | Pricing calculator makes cost concrete for a specific trade and volume. | Observation |
| Opportunity | The post-sale journey (permitting, inspection, utility approval) gets little visual explanation compared with design and sales. | Interpretation |
| Opportunity | Homeowner communication after signing is not described publicly. | Interpretation |

These opportunities are about the **public story**, not the actual product, which I have not used.

## 10. Why this research led to Groundwork

- **[Verified]** Operations and “sale to PTO” are named audiences and outcomes. (S3)
- **[Interpretation]** The post-sale phase is long, depends on outside parties, and is hard for homeowners to follow.
- **[Assumption]** Coordinators manage many projects at once and lack a ranked view of which ones need them today. **This needs user research to confirm.**

That combination gave a product direction that is related to the company’s domain but **does not redesign or copy** its product: an independent concept for the post-sale coordination problem.
